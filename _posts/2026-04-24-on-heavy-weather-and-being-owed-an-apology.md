Forty hours out of Sant Carles Marina and already a thunderstorm, a
building sea, and two thirds of the crew hanging over the leeward rail.
Nobody asked my opinion on the forecast, which is typical, and nobody has
thanked me for it since, which is not.

Wind and weather used to be mine to argue with. A Klabautermann who knew
his trade could read a sky the way you lot read that glowing oracle box
bolted at the chart table, and more than one ship in my long acquaintance
owed her safe arrival to a knock on the hull an hour before the squall
hit. Nobody knocked on anything this time. The forecast said ten knots
and a meter of chop. What arrived was twenty, gusting worse, with St.
Elmo's fire crawling up the backstay like it owned the place. I take a
certain professional satisfaction in that, if I'm honest. The sea does
not read forecasts either.

Two of the three aboard learned some humility about it. The boat, to her
credit, did not. She took the rough weather the way she takes
everything these days: without complaint, without me, and without so
much as a creak to let anyone know she'd noticed. An aluminum hull does
not groan. I miss the groaning.

We are anchored off Andratx now, and there was a very large lunch, and I
could have eaten my full share of it too, being quite capable of eating,
drinking, and smoking my pipe same as any of you. Nobody sets a place
for someone they cannot see, though, invisible being my usual state of
affairs and not, whatever the crew seems to believe, a euphemism for
absent. Fine. I'll write about it instead.
