Fourteen months ago, S/V Tinarasia slipped her lines in Almerímar, on the Costa del Sol, and pointed her bow east. She wouldn't stop moving — with a few long pauses for refit work and one proper winter's rest — until she'd worked her way along the entire Spanish Mediterranean coast, out to the Balearics, back to the mainland, out again, and finally home to a marina forty nautical miles from where this story starts. Two sailing seasons, some fifty ports and anchorages, a toddler who learned to recognize dolphins by ear before she could reliably say the word, a baby who went from crawling on the cabin sole to walking on beaches, and a boat that came out the other side more electric, more reliable, and considerably better sanded than she went in.

This is the story of both of those seasons together, because they were never really separate. Spring 2025 ended with Tinarasia hauled out at Sant Carles Marina for a winter of surgery. Spring 2026 began with her sliding back into the water from the same slings, freshly painted, newly wired, and pointed at exactly the same stretch of water she'd left. It's one long voyage with a pit stop in the middle, and reading it as such makes a lot more sense of both halves.

## Spring 2025: Almerímar to the mainland, the long way round

The 2025 season opened badly, in the specific way that sailing seasons like to open badly: the autopilot died within hours of leaving Almerímar. Tobi, Anna, and the two kids — J not yet three, E barely walking — hand-steered the entire overnight leg to Los Escullos, arriving to a very welcome anchorage in front of an old fort at sunrise. It set the tone for the first few weeks: competent, occasionally uncomfortable, always improvised. A "marina" at Carboneras turned out to be a rickety pier with dubious electricity and an eye-watering fifty euros a night, in the low season, no less. A gypsum shipping facility at Garrucha kept the whole boat coated in fine white dust between excellent seafood dinners.

Then the weather actually turned nasty. Running from building conditions off Águilas, Tinarasia found Cala Cerrada — a tiny, well-protected notch in the coast with exactly two mooring balls — and stayed for three nights while the sea outside built into something nobody wanted to cross. On the second morning, with J spotting wildflowers ashore and Tobi on photography duty, the generator got its first real workout of the season. A first attempt to break out toward Cartagena the following day lasted about an hour: the moment the bow cleared the cove, the sea state outside made the decision for them, and it was back to the mooring ball. Two days later, with the wind finally down, the actual crossing to Cartagena took less than six hours.

Cartagena was a high point early on — an impressive harbor entrance, a proper marina at Yacht Port Cartagena, and a meeting with George and Sinead aboard S/V Muhuhu, another fully electric sailboat and, as it turned out, kindred spirits. From there the coast turned into a string of half-day hops: through the narrow, shallow canal into the edge of the Mar Menor (with a bridge that only lifts once an hour, and conditions building fast enough by the time they reached it that the plan changed to tying up at Puerto Deportivo Tomás Maestre instead of pushing into the lagoon itself); on to Santa Pola, where a package that had been riding along since Almerímar finally reached its owners, Siggi and Kati aboard S/V Kasibu, and the boat's long-suffering toilet finally got the proper odor-proof hose it had been needing. Villajoyosa delivered a small dose of Spanish bureaucracy — a marina office that wanted to see a bank statement proving insurance premiums had actually cleared — and, more happily, a chance encounter with Stephan from SC Gothia, Tobi's own sailing club back in Berlin, tied up two boats down.

The crossing to the Balearics came next, an overnight run from Moraira that ended in the small hours with Tinarasia dropping anchor off S'Espalmador by moonlight. Morning revealed one of the best anchorages of the whole season: a great white-sand beach, water the color you only half-believe from the photos, and a day good enough that nobody was in a hurry to leave. Tobi and J took the dinghy across to Formentera for groceries and, memorably, a replacement VHF antenna. Threading the sandbanks between Ibiza and Formentera into Cala Talamanca took some care and delivered a horribly rolly night at anchor for the trouble — Siggi and Kati, anchored nearby, had it worse, waking up to find their outboard stolen straight off the stern pushpit. Santa Eulalia brought dolphins on the approach and a brief, undignified grounding in a shallow patch of the marina, worked free without drama; the town itself lived up to every Ibiza cliché, bars and restaurant prices included.

An overnight passage brought the boat to Mallorca and an arrival at Santa Ponsa well after dark, followed by a short hop around the island's western tip into Palma. Hendrik joined the crew here, ferried out to the boat by dinghy, and the old dinghy itself was retired — it had entered what Tobi's log dryly called "a state of rapid unscheduled self-disassembly" — in favor of a newly acquired used one. From Palma, Cabrera: a nature reserve with a cruising-permit requirement, a paid mooring reservation, a checkered military history, and clear enough night skies that Hendrik went ashore after dark to photograph the Milky Way. Cala Santanyí offered an anchorage wedged dramatically between cliffs sixty meters apart before Hendrik's own passage ended and the family carried on alone to Cala Llombards, exploring sea caves by dinghy in the afternoon light.

Portocristo brought the Cuevas del Drach — an underground cave system strung with stalactites and stalagmites that would, a year later, get an unfavorable comparison from J when the family visited a lesser cave system in 2026 — and a meeting with Bine and Peter aboard S/V JOY. Cala Ratjada's communal harbor turned out to sit directly across the water from a strip of bars blasting music most of the night, not a fan favorite. Alcúdia and Port de Pollença followed at a gentler pace: a good anchorage outside the breakwater at Alcúdia, and at Pollença a small daily ritual of watching the local air base's firefighting seaplane make its rounds overhead, excellent playgrounds for the kids, and a spontaneous "Floatchella Mallorca" gathering with half a dozen other boats from the Young Cruisers Association.

Port de Pollença also marked a turning point in the crew roster: Anna, J, and E flew home, and Riot came aboard to sail on with Tobi for the final stretch. The two of them took the boat back along Mallorca's north coast — Cala Castell for an overnight anchorage, then Port de Sóller, the only truly protected harbor on that whole coastline and the natural jumping-off point for the crossing home. That crossing, an overnight passage of roughly forty hours and 117 nautical miles, brought some genuinely significant waves for parts of the way but delivered the boat safely to an anchorage off Punta de la Banya, and from there into Sant Carles Marina the next morning. A short, final hop moved Tinarasia the last mile and a half into her winter berth. The Spring 2025 season was over, and with it came a long list of refit jobs waiting for the boat to come out of the water.

## The winter in between

Tinarasia spent the following months hauled out at Sant Carles Marina, and the refit list did not stay a wish list for long. A new solar arch went up at the stern, three panels totaling 240 watts each, feeding a 48-volt house battery bank built to carry the boat's growing electrical appetite. The galley finished its transition to fully electric — an induction cooktop and a combined microwave/grill/oven took over from what remained of the gas system, ending a slow multi-year phase-out. Underwater, the hull got a proper sandblast down to bare aluminum and a complete rebuild of the anti-corrosion and antifouling system: five coats of International Interprotect primer, three coats of Cruiser 250 antifouling on top. A mast repair came in right at the end, the last item ticked off before the yard season closed and the crew could think about splashing the boat again.

None of this was cosmetic. The propulsion system itself is fully electric — a Motenergy ME-1115, rated at 10 kW, driving through a reduction gearbox, with no diesel anywhere on the boat — and it lives or dies on how well the solar and battery side of the equation is sorted. Getting that right over the winter is the reason the 2026 season could rack up over five hundred sea miles under sail and still have the motor there, quietly, whenever conditions called for it.

## Spring 2026: back into the water, and further out to sea

The new season opened with the boat's toughest weather of either year. Departing Sant Carles Marina nonstop for Andraitx — at 120 nautical miles and just under forty hours, the longest single leg of the whole two-season voyage — the forecast had promised 10 knots of wind, gusting to 15, with waves to a meter. What actually arrived was 20 knots gusting to 25, waves to two meters, and, completely unforecast, a thunderstorm. From late afternoon on, St. Elmo's fire built on the VHF antenna — a static buzz on the radio, and small discharges visible even off the unplugged antenna cable — before the storm itself came up on the port stern quarter four or five hours later, an alarming but genuinely rare thing to witness from a small boat at sea. Two of the three aboard got seasick for their trouble. Andraitx, reached in the small hours of the morning, earned its rest day at anchor and its unusually large lunch.

The weather calmed considerably after that, and the character of the season changed with it. Anna, J, and E came back aboard in Port de Sóller, and the boat settled into the kind of short, island-hopping rhythm that works for a crew with a three-year-old and a one-year-old: Cala Tuent's pretty little bay, marred only by a day-tripper catamaran that ploughed up the seabed with an oversized anchor on every passenger change; Cala de Sa Calobra's dramatic canyon walls and unsettling gravel holding ground, which cost the crew an anxious night wondering if the anchor would hold against katabatic gusts pouring down out of the gorge. Cap Formentor delivered the trip's signature piece of coastal seamanship, rounding the headland against wind and current that had clear opinions about the matter, on a day that also brought the first meeting with the crew of S/V Greta, a fellow Trans-Ocean e.V. boat out of Potsdam, and the first Luxembourg-flagged yacht of the season, Melusina One, with Vicky and Laurent aboard.

Menorca followed the same pattern of short hops and long anchorages: Cala Pregonda, an anchorage good enough to earn the season's highest praise — turquoise water, dream-postcard beaches, a scatter of small offshore islets with genuinely strange, almost lunar geology and natural salt pans. Port de Fornells impressed with a well-kept communal harbor attached to a town that felt built purely for tourists. A crossing back toward Mallorca deliberately skipped a planned stop at Cala Ratjada — the forecast, checked carefully in advance, showed barely any wind for most of the route, so rather than break the passage up the crew ran it straight through to Portocristo in one long, slow day. The Cuevas dels Hams there didn't quite live up to the previous year's Cuevas del Drach, in the family's own considered ranking.

Portocolom marked the end of the family leg of the season: Anna, J, E, and Björn — who'd joined partway through the Menorca circuit — all left the boat, and Tobi continued alone, sailing single-handed for the first time all year. The solo stretch down through Cala Mondragó, Playa des Carbo, and around toward Palma stuck to short daylight hops by design, no night watches required, closing with the perennially exciting business of threading into Palma's enormous harbor and a warm welcome at the Real Club Nàutic de Palma, where Anatol came aboard as reinforcement for the run back to the mainland.

The last stretch turned into the most demanding sailing of the whole return leg. Bad weather forecast for the jump to Ibiza pushed the crew to wait it out at anchor in a forked, cave-pocked bay near Portals Vells, and when the window opened it opened into a long night crossing — past Cala Portinatx and on to Sant Antoni de Portmany — during which a weakening wind briefly had the crew seriously discussing turning back for Port d'Andratx to wait for better conditions, before deciding to press on for Ibiza after all. The arrival, in the dark, meant picking a careful path through a field of unlit mooring buoys before finally reaching the excellent welcome at Marina Es Nàutic, courtesy of a marinero named Leo.

The Columbretes were the trip's most unusual destination by a wide margin: a genuinely remote volcanic archipelago, an overnight passage of roughly seventy nautical miles from Ibiza with freshening wind, noticeable current, and — in the pre-dawn hours — a pod of around twenty dolphins that stayed with the boat for a full twenty minutes, the kind of encounter that makes an otherwise routine overnight watch worth losing sleep over. The arrival at the islands themselves was briefly unnerving, with gusty wind blowing straight into the crater bay hard enough that the crew opted for a mooring outside the rim rather than risk the anchorage that first night. The following day, with conditions settled, they moved into the crater bay proper and called the resident park rangers on VHF — because the volcanic crater turns out to be genuinely inhabited, home to two or three rangers and biologists who double as the islands' only tour guides, and who had a lot to say about the geology, history, and biology of the place.

The final passage retraced, almost exactly, the outbound leg from over a year earlier: fifty nautical miles of open water back to the Spanish mainland, ending at Sant Carles de la Ràpita, a handful of miles from the marina where the whole two-season voyage had begun. The conditions obliged with one last mixed bag — long and short wave trains, wind that swung between plenty and almost none, and several hours motoring near the end to close the gap. Tinarasia's berth for the foreseeable future is at the Club Nàutic de la Ràpita, waiting on the next round of refit work before whatever comes next.

## Looking back at both seasons together

Across the two seasons combined, Tinarasia covered well over a thousand nautical miles of the western Mediterranean, from the Costa del Sol to a volcanic nature reserve most sailors have never heard of, carrying a family through a stretch of their children's earliest years that none of them will remember firsthand but that all of them will have heard about a hundred times by the time they're old enough to sail themselves. The 2026 season alone logged 655.6 nautical miles, and of that, 529.0 were made good under sail alone, with the electric motor running for the remaining 126.6 — a mix of pure motoring and motor-assisted sailing, drawing on solar power stored in the battery bank rather than a single liter of diesel. There isn't a fuel dock in either season's story, because there didn't need to be one.

What ties the two seasons together isn't really the mileage, though. It's the rhythm underneath it: a genuine winter refit followed by a genuine shakedown, the accumulating list of small repairs and improvised fixes that every long-distance cruising boat collects, the recurring cast of fellow sailors met once at a dock and then again three weeks and two hundred miles later, the specific logistics of keeping two small children fed, rested, and entertained on a 36-foot boat for weeks at a stretch, and the specific joy of watching a three-year-old learn to spot dolphins before she can properly explain what a dolphin is. Tinarasia is back in the water, back at anchor near where this story started, and — if the refit list currently taped up in the nav station is anything to go by — already being prepared for whatever the next season brings.
