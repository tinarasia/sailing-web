Cala de Sa Calobra has a bottom made of loose gravel and a habit of
funneling gusts straight down the gorge behind it at two in the morning,
which is, incidentally, my shift. Warning a crew that their anchor is
dragging is proper Klabautermann work. It is one of the oldest jobs in
the trade, older than the pipe, nearly as old as the beard.

I did not get to do it. The boat's glowing oracle box did it for me,
chiming away belowdecks before I'd even finished climbing the chain to
check. I am told this is called an anchor alarm. I am told it is very
reliable. I would like it on the record that I was already awake.

The two smallest crew members, meanwhile, continue to outpace me on
sheer capacity for mischief, which stings rather more than the alarm
does. A proper ship's spirit causes gentle chaos: a jammed halyard here,
a misplaced marlinspike there, nothing a sailor can't work around. A
three-year-old and a one-year-old, once let loose on deck, achieve more
disorder before breakfast than I have managed in several centuries of
dedicated effort. I have taken notes.

We are back in Port de Sóller for the ice cream and the little train up
to town, both of which I approve of. I have hands enough to hold a cone
and lungs enough to enjoy the ride, same as anyone, and could do both in
plain sight if I cared to. I simply don't care to. A carriage full of
tourists is not worth breaking a few centuries of good invisibility
over.
