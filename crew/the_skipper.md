... is a mysterious creature, somewhere between agent of chaos incarnate and
demigod of the wind and sea.

Every proper ship, the old sailors say, carries a Klabautermann: a small,
gruff kobold of a spirit, born the moment the timber for her keel or her
mast was felled, and bound to the vessel for as long as she stays afloat.
He lives in the bilges and up in the rigging, mends what needs mending in
the dead of night, warns of storms with a knock on the hull, and is never,
ever seen by the living, except by the one unlucky soul aboard who's
marked to drown. Whistle at him and, if he's in a good mood, he whistles
back. Cross him, mock him, or let the ship go to ruin around him, and he
leaves. A ship a Klabautermann has abandoned is a ship already lost.

Tinarasia's Klabautermann came aboard the day her aluminum was cut, which
he still brings up as though it were a personal slight. There is no
wooden hull to caulk. No bilge worth pumping by hand. No oil lamp to trim,
no rigging that needs him hauling on it at 3 a.m., and now that she carries
a solar arch and an electric motor, nobody even needs him to lean on the
wind to fill the sails. A fully electric boat with a moody but largely
self-sufficient autopilot is, frankly, an underemployed Klabautermann's
worst nightmare.

So he has found a new trade. In his own words:

> "I write snarky blog posts about sailboat cruising in general, and
> Tinarasia's crew in particular, because on modern boats I don't have
> nearly as much to do as I did back in the golden age of sail."

If the trip reports on this site read a little sharper than the average
cruising blog, if the crew page has opinions nobody asked for, that's him:
a centuries-old ship's spirit with nothing left to fix, filling the idle
hours the only way he knows how, by talking about you behind your back,
where you can, technically, read every word.
